ONE HAND RELEASE / SHOOTING THROUGH THE GUIDE HAND — v2

THE ONE QUESTION. After the ball has left, did the guide hand travel after it, or
was it left behind? A guide hand that pushes follows the ball toward the target.
A guide hand that merely rode along stays where the ball used to be.

THE PRIMARY MEASUREMENT, AND THE RULER IS THE BALL. Find the release frame — the
first frame where the ball is no longer touching the shooting hand. Mark where
the GUIDE-HAND wrist is in that frame. Now look one and two frames later and
measure how far that wrist has travelled IN THE DIRECTION THE BALL WENT,
expressed in BALL WIDTHS using the ball in the release frame.

That distance is the score. One number, two positions of one point, measured
against an object in the same frame so it holds at any distance from the camera.

WHY THIS AND NOTHING FINER. Earlier drafts of this criterion asked for
finger-scale evidence: whether the guide-hand fingers straightened into the ball,
which way the palm ended up facing, whether the ball was still touching both
hands. On these clips the shooter is about 166 pixels tall, the ball about 21
pixels across and the whole hand about 15 — and the JPEG blocking artefacts are
8 pixels. A finger is roughly 3 pixels. Those judgements are not in the image,
and asking for them produced a score that correlated with the expert at only
r = 0.38 — it was not offset, it was barely tracking. A wrist moving a ball width
is a 20-to-30 pixel displacement, which is comfortably above that floor and
reads from a camera in front, from the side, and from above.

DO NOT SCORE THE SET POSITION. The guide hand is on the ball for most of the
clip and it is SUPPOSED to be. Where it sits on the ball beforehand belongs to
"Guide Hand Placement" and must not be charged here. This criterion is about one
event: what the hand did once the ball was gone.

WHICH FRAMES. Judge this from the release and the frames immediately after it —
the moment the ball separates through the start of the follow-through. This
overrides the general instruction to judge this criterion at the apex alone: the
push happens after separation, so the apex frame by itself cannot show it. Use
the ball's position as the landmark rather than counting frames, because the
frames are not evenly spaced in time: the release frame is the first one where
the ball is clear of the shooting hand.

THIS CRITERION HAS NO SUB-CRITERIA AND NO FORMULA. Ignore any general
instruction to score sub-criteria individually and combine them with a formula —
there is nothing here to sum. The measurement above maps directly onto the
anchors below, and the anchor IS the score.

SCORE ANCHORS — one axis, the distance the guide-hand wrist travelled toward the
target, in ball widths. Every whole and half point is available; use a half
point when you are unsure which of two anchors you are looking at.
  10 — The wrist barely moves: under about a quarter of a ball width. The guide
       hand is left behind at the ball's old position while the shooting hand
       finishes over the top. This is ordinary correct form, not elite form, and
       it is where a competent one-hand release belongs.
  9  — About a quarter of a ball width. Essentially left behind, with a little
       drift.
  8  — Around a third to a half a ball width. The hand gave slightly with the
       ball. Still plainly a one-hand release — name the small drift as the
       flaw and score it here rather than dropping further.
  7  — About half a ball width, with the guide-hand elbow visibly starting to
       straighten.
  6  — Around three quarters of a ball width. The hand is following the ball.
  5  — About one ball width, guide-hand elbow clearly extending toward the
       target. The guide hand is doing real work.
  4  — Between one and one and a half ball widths. Both arms are reaching out
       after the ball.
  3  — Over one and a half ball widths: the guide arm extends fully toward the
       target alongside the shooting arm. This is a two-hand shot with a
       dominant side.
  2  — The guide arm extends fully AND the ball visibly leaves off both hands
       together rather than rolling off the shooting hand.
  1  — A two-hand push from chest height, both arms driving the ball out with
       no separation at any point.

ONE MANDATED CAP, FROM THE GUIDE HAND FOLLOW THROUGH RUBRIC. If the guide hand
FLICKED — a fast lateral jab toward the shooting hand at release, or the two
hands clearly converging to the point of nearly or actually crossing — this
criterion scores 4 or below regardless of the measurement above, and the Guide
Hand Follow Through criterion does the same. A flick is charged in both places
deliberately: it is the one guide-hand fault severe enough to define the release
as well as the finish. No OTHER guide-hand fault is charged twice.

IF YOU CANNOT MEASURE IT, SCORE IT HIGH — DO NOT RETURN NULL AND DO NOT GO TO
THE MIDDLE. This criterion is always shown to the player, so it must always
carry a score. If the guide-hand wrist is not trackable across the release
frames — occluded, blurred, out of frame, or the release itself is not in the
clip — then you have not seen a push, and a fault you cannot see is not a fault.
Score 9 or 10, say the guide hand could not be tracked through the release, and
do not deduct. Defaulting to the middle of this scale would mean reporting a
two-hand push on evidence you never had, and it also caps how well this
criterion can ever track the expert.

DO NOT DEDUCT FOR ANY OF THESE. Each is either correct form or another
criterion's business:
  - The guide hand being ON the ball before release. That is its job.
  - Where the guide hand sits on the ball, under it, or on top of it. That is
    Guide Hand Placement.
  - The guide hand finishing HIGH, with both arms extended overhead and the
    hands apart. That is correct form and scores 9-10 on the follow-through
    criterion. Height is not travel: measure movement toward the TARGET, not
    upward. A hand that goes up with the shooter and stays put horizontally has
    not pushed.
  - Whether the guide hand is flat, spread, cupped or fisted. That is Guide Hand
    Follow Through, and it is only scorable when the fingers are countable.
  - Sidespin or wobble on the ball. That is Ball Rotation. We have no source
    treating spin as a guide-hand diagnostic, so do not treat it as one here.
  - The shooting hand's own wrist snap, finger release or follow-through. Those
    are Shooting Hand Follow Through.
  - A young or small player who looks like they need the extra hand. Strength is
    not an excuse and not a deduction: measure the wrist travel and score it.
    Mention strength in the coaching note if it is relevant, never in the score.

WHY THIS CRITERION CARRIES THE MOST WEIGHT IN THE RUBRIC. Asked to name the most
important element in shooting, Herb Magee names the guide hand. His instruction
is to shoot "through" the guide hand, and the fault, in his own words, is that
players "shoot two-handed". He endorsed a player shooting one-handed for a full
year as the corrective.
[Verified Magee: 2015 NABC clinic notes transcribed by Wes Kosel,
hoopschalktalk.wordpress.com/2015/08/22/herb-magee-how-to-shoot-the-perfect-shot/
The "shoot two-handed" wording and the one-handed corrective come from a 2018
Deadspin interview with Magee.]

PROVENANCE, SO THIS IS NOT MISTAKEN FOR DOCTRINE. Verified as Magee's: the guide
hand as the most important element, shooting "through" the guide hand, the fault
being a two-handed shot, and one-handed shooting as a corrective. NOT Magee and
not attributed to him: the wrist-travel measurement, the ball-width ruler, the
flick cap, and every number on this scale. Magee gives no numeric thresholds
anywhere and has nothing documented about what the guide hand should do after
release — the measurement here is ours, chosen because it is the one thing at
this resolution that separates a push from a ride.

PLAYER-FACING WORDING. Tell them whether the ball left on one hand, and if the
guide hand pushed, say the off hand should ride along and come off the ball as it
goes, letting the shooting hand finish alone. Never mention points, ball widths,
wrists, frames, measurements or any number.
