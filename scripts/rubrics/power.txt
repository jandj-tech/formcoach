POWER RUBRIC v6 — did the ball ride up on the legs, or did the arms lift it after the legs were done?

THIS CRITERION IS ABOUT ORDER, NOT DEPTH. How far the knees bend is a weak signal — strong and older shooters shoot from a shallow bend and are still leg-driven. What separates a leg-driven shot from an arm shot is WHEN things happen relative to each other, and that is readable at any distance because it is a comparison between frames, not a measurement inside one.

THE SHAPE OF THE SHOOTING ARM IS NOT SCORED HERE. Elbow position belongs to the elbow criterion and is not charged twice. The only way the arms enter this score is the catapult and the two-hand shove, and those are decided by the set-point check or the flags, not by this guide.

FIND FIVE FRAMES. Track the top of the head and the ball.
  D — the frame where the head is LOWEST in the gather.
  B — the first frame the ball starts rising toward the set point.
  T — the last frame with both feet flat on the floor (or, on a set shot, the frame the heels lift).
  R — the release: the first frame the ball is no longer touching the shooting hand.
  P — the frame where the head is HIGHEST.
If the clip starts after D or ends before R, say so and return null.

THEN ANSWER THREE QUESTIONS FROM THOSE FRAMES:
  1. Did the ball and the body start up TOGETHER? (B is D or the frame after D.) Or did the ball start up while the head was still level or still going down — the arms leading the legs?
  2. Was the body STILL RISING at the release? (R comes before P, and the head is higher in R than in the frame before it.) Or had the body already stopped or started down before the ball left?
  3. Did the ball ever PARK? A ball that sits still at the set point for two or more frames while the head keeps moving means the legs finished and then the arm pushed on its own. One frame of stillness is not a park.

SCORE ANCHORS — land between anchors when the shot sits between them.
  10 — Any visible head drop into the gather; ball and body start up together; body still rising at the release; no park. One motion, legs to fingertips.
  9  — As 10 with one small thing: a very shallow drop, or a single-frame stall at the top.
  8  — A defined set point reached right at takeoff and launched from at once — no park, body still rising at the release. This is a good two-motion shooter, and it is common in strong players.
  7  — Legs and arm both work, but they are SEPARATED: the head has stopped rising, or the feet are flat and still, in the release frame and the one before it. The legs loaded; the arm finished alone. Release still before the peak.
  6  — The legs extend, then the ball parks for two or more frames, then a push; or a long shot released after the peak, on the way down.
  5  — Barely any head drop; the ball rises from a nearly still body; the arm is doing the lifting, but it is one clean one-hand release.
  4  — No head drop and the knees at one angle through the whole rise: the arms lifted it. Also the ceiling when the set-point check reports a CATAPULT.
  2-3 — Two-hand chest shove: the ball never loads above the chin and both arms push it out together, hands mirrored on its sides.
  1  — No lower-body change at any point, and a two-hand shove.

ONE NAMED DEDUCTION: if the guide hand is still on the ball in the release frame and visibly pushing or steering it, take 1 off the anchor you landed on. The expert's own words for that case, scored 6: "the guide hand is doing some work on the ball, which adds power. it should all come from your legs."

WHAT IS NOT A FAULT: a shallow dip in a strong shooter; a set shot with the heels down as long as the knees are straightening through the rise; releasing at the top of the jump on a close shot; a ball held high in front of the forehead with ONE elbow under it and the legs still finishing — that is a high set point, not a catapult, and it scores 8-10 here; landing ahead of the take-off spot; the guide hand on the ball at the set point; both arms finishing high.

DO NOT USE THE WORD CATAPULT unless the set-point check or a flag has established the ball went over or behind the head with both arms. A flared elbow is the elbow criterion's business and costs nothing here.

RETURN NULL WHEN the knees and head are hidden or blurred in every pre-release frame, or every frame you have is at or after the release.

PLAYER-FACING WORDING: tell them to let the ball ride up on the legs — start the ball up as the legs start up, and let it go while the body is still rising, not after it has stopped. Never mention frames, letters, angles, studies or scoring bands in the reasoning.
